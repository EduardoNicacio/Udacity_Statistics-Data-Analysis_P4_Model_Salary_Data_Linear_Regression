## 1. Elevator Pitch

**The Problem:** A tech company wants to know what actually drives salary: is it years of experience, education, job title, gender — or a mixture of all four? The evidence is a single CSV of 375 employees with six columns, two of them categorical in ways regression cannot eat directly (`Gender`, `Education Level`), and one of them a free-text `Job Title` that has to be converted into usable flags. The notebook asks a fixed sequence of questions: describe the data, clean it, group it, build the first OLS model, strip out the noise, and interpret what remains in dollars and confidence intervals.

**The Solution:** The notebook was completed cell by cell in the order the brief specifies, with comments explaining both what each block computes and why. Part I establishes the shape of the data (375 × 6, two fully blank rows → 373 complete observations, salary $350–$250,000) and its distribution (histogram, education counts). Part II groups the data three ways (gender, education, experience), reuses the pre-completed job-title flags, creates `is_male`, dummy-codes education with Bachelor's as the baseline (`is_masters`, `is_phd`), and fits the first 10-feature OLS model with `statsmodels`. Part III filters the coefficients by p-value *programmatically* (p ≤ 0.05, not by eye), refits the reduced model, and answers every interpretation question with a coefficient, a 95% confidence interval, and a plain-English reading — plus a point prediction, its residual, R², and a residuals-vs-fitted diagnostic.

**The Result:** The reduced model explains **91.5%** of salary variation (R² 0.9145, adjusted R² 0.9128) with eight features. Each additional year of experience is worth **+$5,104** (95% CI [$4,755.93, $5,452.95]); a senior title is worth **+$14,446** (95% CI [$10,867, $18,026]); a PhD is worth **+$23,081** over a Bachelor's (95% CI [$17,651.90, $28,509.80]). Three title flags — junior, analyst, engineer — were **not** significant (p = 0.068 / 0.933 / 0.508) and were removed. A male senior engineer with 5 years of experience and a Bachelor's is predicted at **$76,359.11**; against an actual $110,000 the residual is **+$33,640.89**. The residual plot fans out, so the model's point estimates are useful but its default confidence intervals should be read with caution. An independent standard-library re-implementation reproduced every reported figure: **109 checks, 0 failures**. The presentation deck still needs to be filled — it is the one outstanding deliverable.

### Key Design Decisions

| Decision | Rationale |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Follow the cell order, never reorder** | The parts build on each other: the dummies in II f) need the cleaned frame from I c), and Part III needs the fitted `model` to read p-values from. Keeping the given order means every cell can assume exactly what the brief intended it to assume. |
| **Drop the two blank rows, don't impute** | Both problem rows are empty in *all six* columns, so there is nothing to impute from — a mean fill would invent employees who do not exist. `df.dropna()` loses no partial information, and the row count before (375) and after (373) is printed so the change is visible rather than silent. |
| **Comments live in code, not in new markdown cells** | The brief asks for "brief markdown comments" on II a)–c) but also says "do not add cells". Inline `# Comment:` lines honour both: the observation is next to the evidence that produced it, and the cell count stays intact. |
| **Dummy variables with an explicit baseline** | `pd.get_dummies(drop_first=True)` on `Education Level` drops Bachelor's alphabetically-first by construction, giving exactly two columns (`Master's`, `PhD`), which are renamed `is_masters`/`is_phd`. The interpretation of every education coefficient is then "versus a Bachelor's", which is what III c) asks for. |
| **Significance filtered by code, not by reading the table** | `model.pvalues <= 0.05` selects the kept features, so the dropped set (`is_junior`, `is_analyst`, `is_engineer`) is *derived* from the same numbers the summary prints. A human misreading a p-value column cannot silently change the model, and the dropped list is both printed and written in markdown. |
| **Prediction row aligned with `reindex(columns=...)`** | The single-row input is built as a dict and then reindexed onto `model2.params.index` with `fill_value=0`. Column order can never silently misalign, and a flag that no longer exists in the model (e.g. `is_engineer`) is simply absent rather than raising an error — which is exactly the honest behaviour, since the engineer premium was not significant. |
| **Features cast to `float` before OLS** | The flag columns are bool/int as the brief specifies; casting the design matrix to float makes the dtype contract explicit for `statsmodels` and avoids version-dependent bool handling. |
| **Age excluded, per the brief's feature list** | The model uses exactly the features `a`–`e` name (gender, job-title flags, experience, education). Age correlates with salary at r ≈ 0.92 but sits in near-collinearity with `Years of Experience`; adding it would destabilise the very coefficients the brief asks us to interpret. Recorded as future work instead. |
| **Report the model the data support** | Where the residual diagnostics contradict a textbook "good fit" (the funnel), the written answer says so and proposes the fix (HC3 robust SEs / log-Salary) rather than repeating R² = 0.915 as if it settled the question. |

## 3. Analytical Principles in Practice

**A coefficient is a conditional expectation difference, and the text says "holding constant".** Every interpretation names what is held fixed — III a) holds gender, title flags, and education constant; III c) holds everything else constant. "Each additional year is worth $5,104" is only true *for people who are otherwise identical*, and the notebook never drops that qualifier.

**The baseline travels with the number.** `is_phd = +$23,081` means "versus a Bachelor's with no PhD and no Master's", because Bachelor's was the dropped dummy. Reading it as "versus everyone else" would overstate the gap by ~$14k (the Master's premium). The answer cell names the baseline explicitly.

**Significance and practical size are reported together.** `is_manager` is significant (p = 0.019) but worth only **+$4,285** — a real but small effect; `is_director` is worth **+$24,981**. Conversely `is_junior` (p = 0.068) is *not* evidence of "no junior discount"; it is an undetectable effect at this sample size. The notebook reports p-values and dollar magnitudes side by side so neither is read alone.

**A 95% CI is a range for the true effect, not a range for the data.** III a)'s [$4,755.93, $5,452.95] is the interval of per-year increases compatible with the model at 95% confidence — and it excludes zero, which is *why* the effect is significant. The same logic runs through III b) and III c); each written answer states the interval and what believing it commits you to.

**R² is not a certificate of specification.** 91.5% of variance explained coexists with a visibly misspecified error structure: the residuals fan out, so high-salary employees are predicted with far more uncertainty than junior ones. The fit metric and the residual plot answer different questions, and the notebook reports both.

**Residuals diagnose, and the diagnosis has consequences.** The funnel (residual SD ≈ $7.3k in the bottom fitted quartile → ≈ $20k in the top) means homoscedasticity is violated, so the default OLS standard errors — and therefore the p-values and CIs quoted above — are approximate. The honest remedy (HC3 robust covariance, or modelling `log(Salary)`) is stated in the notebook rather than left implicit.

**Correlation is not causation, and grouping is not adjustment.** The gender gap ($103,868 vs $97,011) is a raw average; in the adjusted model `is_male` is +$7,630 *conditional on* experience, title, and education. The two numbers answer different questions (descriptive disparity vs. conditional gap), and the notebook keeps them in their own cells.

**Outliers are disclosed, not deleted.** A $350 salary sits 30+ standard errors below the mean and several six-figure salaries sit far above the fit. The brief says drop missing rows, not suspicious ones, so they stay — and the residual discussion records their influence instead of quietly trimming them.

## 4. AI Collaboration Playbook

The deliverable is a notebook whose every figure is graded, plus a deck that must agree with it. The workflow therefore ran on an evidence hierarchy: **dataset → executed notebook → independent recomputation → documentation**.

1. **Plan before editing.** The full cell map (which cells were empty, which were pre-filled, which were answer stubs) was inspected first and the plan for each part was agreed before a single cell changed. That prevented the common failure of "fill everything, then discover the pre-filled bar chart depends on a frame you never cleaned".
2. **Treat the rubric as the acceptance test.** `docs/3. Rubric.md` lists functionality, data preparation, interpretation, value correctness, and the deck. Each was checked against the executed artifact — not against the intention behind the code.
3. **Execute, don't eyeball.** After the edits, the notebook was run top-to-bottom with a fresh kernel (`nbconvert --execute --inplace`, exit 0), and the saved file was parsed programmatically to confirm 19/19 execution counts, zero error outputs, zero warning outputs, and four embedded figures. Passing a visual read of the source is not the same as running it.
4. **Recompute independently.** A second implementation using only `csv` and numpy — no pandas, no statsmodels — rebuilt the design matrix from the raw file, solved the normal equations with `numpy.linalg.lstsq`, derived p-values from a hand-coded incomplete-beta t-distribution, and compared everything against the notebook's printed outputs and markdown answers: **109 checks, 0 failures**. Agreement between two independent code paths is evidence; agreeing with oneself is not.
5. **Check the narrative against the artifact.** Every number in this document and in the README was taken from the executed notebook or the verifier's output. Where a written answer states a rounded figure ($5,104), the verifier confirms the unrounded value rounds to it.
6. **Review as a separate pass.** A dedicated review of the completed notebook against the brief found one real gap (Part III.1's required markdown cell was only a printed line) plus two hygiene issues (nbformat ids, a stale README from a different project). All were fixed and the notebook re-executed before this document was written.
7. **Keep the deck and notebook in lockstep — or say so.** The deck was *not* touched, so instead of pretending consistency, the README marks the presentation as **outstanding** and provides the exact figures to paste in.

The operating principle: **the executed output is the source of truth, and a second implementation is the check on it.** The review pass accepted no figure because the drafting pass asserted it; it recomputed it, and it recorded the places where the specification and the workbook contradicted each other instead of silently choosing one.

## 5. Evaluation & Verification Philosophy

**Executed outputs are the evidence.** Every reported figure — 375 rows, 12 missing cells, 373 after `dropna()`, 224/98/51 education counts, $350/$250,000 salary bounds, the gender and education means, r = 0.930338, all 11 full-model coefficients, the dropped set, all 8 reduced-model coefficients, R² 0.914461 / 0.912820, the $76,359.11 prediction, the $33,640.89 residual — was read from a completed run of the notebook, not estimated or recalled.

**Two implementations, different libraries.** The verification pass parses the CSV with `csv.DictReader` and builds the design matrix with integer arithmetic; the notebook uses pandas dtypes, `get_dummies`, and `statsmodels`. A pandas-specific mistake (a mis-specified `contains`, a dummy baseline falling the wrong way, an index-alignment slip) cannot hide in both code paths at once — and the t-distribution p-values were derived from the regularized incomplete beta function rather than taken from statsmodels, so the p > 0.05 filter was confirmed by an independent route.

**Rounding is presentation only.** All arithmetic runs at full precision; markdown answers round at the cent ($5,104.44) or the dollar ($14,446). The verifier compares displayed strings against independently computed values at the matching precision, so a rounded answer in prose is checked against the unrounded number in the model.

**Assumptions are checked where the brief asks, and flagged where it does not.** Homoscedasticity is tested with the residuals-vs-fitted plot the brief requires; normality is visible in the summary's Jarque-Bera/Omnibus statistics; multicollinearity and functional form were not formally tested and are listed as limitations rather than implied to be fine.

**The verifier is code, and it can lie.** The 109 checks compare *agreement* between two computations of the same specification — they cannot detect a specification error (for example, if the brief's feature list were wrong). That is why the written interpretations are also hand-read for direction, baseline, and units: a coefficient can be numerically perfect and still be described backwards.

**Limits are disclosed.** Two blank rows were dropped rather than imputed; a $350 salary and several $200k+ salaries remain in the data; the gender and education gaps are descriptive, not causal; the confidence intervals rest on a homoscedasticity assumption the data violate; and the presentation deck is unfinished. All of these appear in §6, §9, and the README rather than being buried.

## 6. Challenges & Resolutions

### Challenge 1 - Two rows are blank in every column

**What happened:** `df.isnull().sum()` reports 2 missing values in each of the six columns — 12 in total. Inspection shows this is two *entirely* blank rows (data rows 173 and 261), not scattered missingness: no row is partially filled, so there is no "half an employee" to recover.

**Resolution:** The brief says to drop rows with NaNs, and with nothing to impute from that is also the right call: `df.dropna()` takes 375 → 373 and the row count is printed so the change is visible. Because the rows are completely empty, no information — not even a usable `Years of Experience` without a `Salary` — is lost. The before/after counts appear in the notebook, the README, and §9.

### Challenge 2 - A $350 salary and a $250,000 salary

**What happened:** The minimum salary in the cleaned data is **$350** and the maximum is **$250,000**. The histogram shows a long right tail and an isolated bar near zero. A $350 "annual salary" is almost certainly a data-entry artefact (perhaps a daily rate or a truncated field), and it drags the mean down while inflating the residual variance for junior employees.

**Resolution:** The brief authorises dropping *missing* rows, not suspicious ones, so the value stays — silently deleting observations because they look wrong would be researcher degrees of freedom, not cleaning. Its influence is disclosed instead: §9 records the range, the 3f residual discussion notes the skew and the positive outliers, and "model `log(Salary)`" is the proposed remedy (a log scale compresses exactly this kind of tail).

### Challenge 3 - The profile is a "senior engineer" but the engineer flag was dropped

**What happened:** III d) asks for the expected salary of "a male employee with 5 years of experience as a senior engineer with a bachelor's degree", but `is_engineer` was one of the three flags removed for p > 0.05 (p = 0.508) in III.1. The requested profile contains a feature the final model no longer has.

**Resolution:** The prediction is built from `model2`'s own columns via `reindex(columns=model2.params.index, fill_value=0)` — so `is_engineer` is absent *by construction*, not by a hard-coded omission that could drift out of sync with the model. The written answer states the prediction ($76,359.11) **and** explains that the engineer flag was insignificant and dropped, meaning the model prices this person as any other senior, non-director, non-manager employee. The alternative — refusing the question or refitting a different model — would have broken the brief's own III.1 → III d) chain.

### Challenge 4 - The residuals fan out (heteroscedasticity)

**What happened:** The residuals-vs-fitted plot the brief requests shows a clear funnel: residual spread grows from ≈ $7.3k SD in the lowest fitted quartile to ≈ $20k in the highest, and `corr(|resid|, fitted) ≈ 0.29`. The model is much better at predicting junior salaries than senior ones, which is exactly what homoscedasticity forbids. The summary's Jarque-Bera (p < 0.001) also flags non-normal, right-skewed residuals.

**Resolution:** The III f) answer reports R² (0.9145 / 0.9128) *and* the funnel, states that the constant-variance assumption appears violated, and notes the consequence: default OLS standard errors — and therefore the p-values and confidence intervals quoted in III a)–c) — are not fully reliable. The proposed fixes (HC3 robust covariance, or `log(Salary)`) are named in the notebook and repeated in §10. Reporting only R² would have been the numerically true but statistically misleading answer.

### Challenge 5 - The brief forbids new cells and also requires them

**What happened:** The execution requirements say "do not add cells; fill the existing empty cells in order", but two required artifacts have no cell to live in: III d) and III e) need *code* (`model2.predict()`, `110000 - predicted_salary`) and the starter only provides markdown answer stubs, and III.1 explicitly demands "a markdown cell listing which variables were dropped and why" where no such cell exists.

**Resolution:** The contradiction was surfaced to the human rather than papered over, and the approved fix was **three minimal insertions**: one code cell before each of the III d)/III e) answer stubs, and one markdown cell after the refit listing the dropped flags with their p-values. Every *other* cell — including all pre-filled cells (I a, I d's chart, II d) and every question prompt — was left byte-for-byte untouched, and the insertions are documented in the README's "Review findings". For II a)–c) the other "markdown comment" request was satisfied with inline code comments, which keeps the cell count intact where no cell conflict existed.

### Challenge 6 - The presentation deck is still a template

**What happened:** The rubric grades *both* the notebook and the presentation, and `docs/2. Presentation Components.md` specifies three content areas (data description, regression results, interpretation). The workspace `.pptx` still contains the template's red placeholders ("[Your description of the number of employees…]", "REMOVE BEFORE SUBMITTING"), and no PDF exists.

**Resolution:** This is recorded honestly rather than glossed over: the README's rubric table marks the presentation criterion ❌ **Outstanding**, the pipeline diagram ends at "template not filled", and the README's "Key results" table contains every figure the three slides need, ready to paste. The deck was left untouched because filling it is a separate, explicitly scoped deliverable (and because the original analysis task said the presentation was out of scope). It is the single item blocking submission.

## 10. Retrospective

### What I'd Do Differently

1. **Fill the deck first, not last.** The rubric grades the presentation, and the one outstanding item is the only thing blocking submission. Writing the "Key results" table *before* finishing the notebook — then checking both against each other — would have made deck/notebook drift impossible instead of merely unlikely.
2. **Report HC3 robust standard errors alongside the default ones.** The funnel is visible in the required plot, so the notebook could print both covariance variants in III.1 and quote the robust intervals in III a)–c). That converts a caveat into a checked result.
3. **Test the three dropped flags jointly.** Each was dropped on its individual p-value; a joint F-test of `is_junior = is_analyst = is_engineer = 0` would answer whether the *set* carries signal (an individual p = 0.068 can still be part of a jointly meaningful group). Multiple-comparison adjustment (e.g. Bonferroni on the 10 tests) is the same family of concern.
4. **Run a multicollinearity check (VIF) and a functional-form check.** Experience and Age correlate at ≈ 0.92, and the brief excludes Age — but that near-collinearity should be *measured*, not assumed away. A RESET-style check or a quadratic `Years` term would test the linear form the scatter only suggests.
5. **Model `log(Salary)` as a parallel specification.** The funnel and the $350/$250,000 tail both point that way: a log fit would likely restore constant variance and turn the dollar coefficients into percentage premiums (≈ "each year adds ~5%"), which is often the more communicable quantity for a slide deck.

### What Worked Well

1. **An independent verifier with different libraries.** Rebuilding 109 values from `csv` + `numpy.linalg.lstsq` — including t-distribution p-values from a hand-coded incomplete beta — means a pandas or statsmodels-specific slip cannot hide in both paths. All 109 passed.
2. **Executing instead of reading.** `nbconvert --execute` with a fresh kernel, followed by programmatic parsing of the saved outputs (execution counts, error cells, warning streams, embedded images), turns "it should run" into a checked fact.
3. **Filtering significance in code.** Deriving the reduced model from `model.pvalues <= 0.05` rather than from a human reading of the table made the dropped set reproducible and reviewable; the printed list and the markdown list are generated from the same numbers.
4. **Reindexing the prediction row.** Building the III d) input against `model2.params.index` with `fill_value=0` meant the dropped `is_engineer` flag could not cause a `NameError` or a silent column-order bug — the code and the model cannot disagree.
5. **Keeping questions and prompts byte-identical.** Every markdown question, every pre-filled cell (I a, the bar chart, II d) was left untouched; answers live in new or emptied cells, so a reviewer can always see the original brief next to the evidence.
6. **Documenting the specification's contradictions.** The "no new cells" rule vs. the required markdown cell, and the "markdown comment" request vs. the same rule, were surfaced to the human and resolved with three minimal insertions — recorded in three places rather than smuggled in.

### Final Thoughts

The modelling itself is deliberately ordinary — clean, dummy-code, OLS, filter by p-value, interpret — because the course is teaching *reading* a regression, not inventing one. The judgement shows up in three places: naming the baseline every coefficient is relative to (Bachelor's, no title, female); reading a 95% CI as a statement about the *true* effect rather than the sample; and refusing to let R² = 0.915 close the question when the residual plot visibly disagrees.

The most useful lesson came from the gap between "significant" and "understood". Three title flags fell out at p > 0.05, and the honest reading is that the data cannot see their effect — not that junior titles pay nothing. Likewise the funnel in the residuals means the tidy confidence intervals are approximations, and saying so is part of the answer, not a footnote to it. Verifying every figure against a second implementation protected the numbers; reading the residual plot against the R² protected the interpretation; and writing the outstanding deck item into the rubric table protects the reader from assuming the project is finished when the zip still needs a PDF.
